# STE Benefit Copy — Full Guide

Write and audit user-facing copy with two disciplines applied together:

1. **ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English** — a controlled language spec (53 writing rules + an approved dictionary of ~900 words) built so a reader with weak English gets exactly one meaning from a sentence. It kills ambiguity.
2. **Benefit-first framing** — the reader does not want the capability. They want what it gives them back: freedom, peace of mind, time, power. Technical capability is evidence, never the headline.

STE alone produces copy that is correct and dead. Benefit framing alone produces copy that is warm and vague. Do both.

## When to Use

Any user-facing text: landing pages, hero sections, headlines, feature blurbs, onboarding, empty states, error messages, tooltips, docs, release notes, emails, product descriptions. Also whenever the user mentions STE, ASD-STE100, controlled language, plain English, readability, "make this clearer", "tighten this copy", "this sounds too technical", or benefit-driven messaging. Trigger even when the user only asks for "some copy".

## The Workflow

### Step 1: Classify the copy

The rules tighten or loosen depending on what the text is. Pick one mode:

| Mode | What it covers | Sentence cap | Voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Procedural** | Steps, instructions, setup guides, error recovery, safety notes | 20 words | Imperative, active only. No "you". |
| **Descriptive** | Docs, explainers, tooltips, release notes, changelogs | 25 words | Active. Third person or "you". |
| **Marketing** | Headlines, hero, feature blurbs, emails, landing pages | 25 words (headlines: 12) | Active, second person ("you") |

Procedural and descriptive copy gets **strict STE**. Marketing copy gets **STE-informed** — every mechanical rule still applies, but the four benefit primitives (below) are allowed as nouns even though they are not in the STE dictionary. This is a deliberate, bounded deviation. Do not widen it.

### Step 2: Run the checker

```bash
python scripts/check_copy.py <file> --mode marketing
```

Modes: `procedural`, `descriptive`, `marketing`. Pass `-` to read stdin. Add `--json` for machine output.

It flags sentence length, paragraph length, passive voice, `-ing` forms, noun clusters, unapproved words with substitutions, and marketing filler. It is a fast mechanical pass, not a judge — it catches maybe 70% of violations. Read the copy yourself for the rest.

If no file exists yet, write the draft first, then check it. Never ship copy that has not been through the checker.

### Step 3: Fix mechanically

Work through the checker output. The rules that matter most, in order of how often they are broken:

- **One word, one meaning.** Each word carries a single approved sense across the whole document. If "monitor" is a noun somewhere, it is never a verb elsewhere. One term per concept, never varied for style — "sync", "synchronize", and "update" are three words, so they must be three different things.
- **One idea per sentence.** Split on every "and" that joins two thoughts. Split on every semicolon.
- **Active voice, verb early.** "The report is generated by the system" → "The system makes the report."
- **Simple tenses only.** Present, simple past, simple future. No perfect, no continuous. "We have been building" → "We build."
- **No `-ing` forms** unless part of a fixed technical name. "Booking engine" is a name and stays. "By connecting your calendar, you get..." → "Connect your calendar. You get..."
- **Max 3 nouns in a row.** "Property management channel sync failure" → "The channel sync failed for a property."
- **Keep articles and "that".** Do not compress by deleting function words. "Ensure setting enabled" → "Make sure that the setting is on."
- **Approved words only.** See the substitution tables below and `references/word-swaps.md`. The official ASD-STE100 Dictionary (free from asd-ste100.org) is the authority on contested words.
- **Numbers, not adjectives.** "Fast sync" is unverifiable. "Sync in 30 seconds" is a claim.

### Step 4: Benefit pass

The part the checker cannot do. For every headline, section lead, and feature bullet, run the "so what" chain:

```
Feature → what it does → what it removes or gives → primitive
```

Stop when you hit one of the four primitives. **Three hops maximum.** If the chain runs past three hops, the feature is too deep to lead with — put it in the docs. If it reaches no primitive at all, the feature does not go in the copy. This is the useful part of the method: it deletes things.

#### The four primitives

- **Freedom** — the reader stops depending on someone. Signals: you do it yourself, you own it, you leave whenever you want, no contract, no phone call.
- **Peace of mind** — a bad outcome the reader was carrying is now impossible or handled. Signals: nothing breaks silently, nothing double-books, nothing is lost, you find out before the customer does, you can stop checking.
- **Time back** — hours returned, in a unit the reader recognizes. Signals: minutes instead of days, one place instead of six tabs, done while you sleep, never again.
- **Power** — the reader can now do something that was out of reach, or beat someone who used to beat them. Signals: compete with the big operator, see what they see, change it yourself, at any scale.

Two secondary primitives are allowed only when literally true and specific: **money kept** (a number, not "savings") and **standing** (the reader looks competent to their own customers). Do not reach for these first.

#### Structure of a benefit line

1. **Lead sentence lands on the primitive.** Concrete, second person, present tense, under 12 words for a headline.
2. **Second sentence gives the mechanism.** The technical capability appears here, as evidence.
3. **Third sentence gives the number**, if there is one.

Never invert this. A capability first with a benefit bolted on ("...so you can focus on what matters") reads as an apology for the first half of the sentence.

#### Worked examples

- Before: "Automated two-way ARI sync across 40+ channel partners with sub-minute propagation."
  Chain: rates and availability match everywhere → no double bookings → **peace of mind**
  After: "You never take two bookings for the same night. Rates and availability match on all 40 channels, in less than a minute."

- Before: "Our AI-powered dispute engine leverages machine learning to optimize letter generation."
  Chain: writes the letters → you do not research bureau rules → you do it yourself without paying anyone → **freedom**, **time back**
  After: "You do not need to pay a repair company. Write your dispute letters in ten minutes. We know the rules; you keep the money."

### Step 5: Output

Deliver in this exact order:

```
## Audit
[table: line/snippet | rule broken | fix]

## Rewritten copy
[the complete text, top to bottom, ready to paste — never a diff, never a fragment,
never "...rest unchanged". If it is a file, output the whole file.]

## Notes
[claims that need verification, terms that need a glossary entry, anything
where STE and clarity actually conflicted and why you chose what you chose]
```

Complete output is not optional. Partial snippets and diffs get mis-assembled and cause real damage.

## STE Rules Condensed

The spec has nine rule sections. Full treatment in `references/ste-rules.md`; the essentials:

**Words.** Approved words only, in the approved part of speech, with the approved meaning. One concept, one word — elegant variation is the single most common cause of reader confusion. Technical names are exempt but must be defined once and used identically. Never use a technical name as a verb ("Onboard the customer" → "Set up the customer"). No slang, idiom, or metaphor a non-native reader must decode ("out of the box", "under the hood", "heavy lifting" all fail). *Relaxed for marketing:* benefit nouns allowed; one culturally neutral metaphor per page.

**Noun phrases.** Max three nouns in a row — break stacks with prepositions and articles. Keep articles and "that". Avoid stacked possessives and chains of "of".

**Verbs.** Approved forms: infinitive, imperative, simple present/past/future, past participle as adjective. No `-ing` outside fixed names. Active voice everywhere — passive hides the actor. Verb early. Do not turn verbs into nouns ("perform a calculation of" → "calculate").

**Sentences.** Word caps per mode (20/25/25, headlines 12). One idea per sentence. Vary length deliberately — twelve consecutive short sentences read like a robot. Front-load the topic. Vertical list for more than three parallel items. No parentheses smuggling in a second sentence.

**Procedures.** One instruction per step; a step with two verbs is two steps. Start with the imperative verb. Condition before action: "If the sync fails, open the log." Reason in a separate sentence when the step is not obvious.

**Descriptive writing.** Six sentences max per paragraph; web copy: three. One topic per paragraph, announced in the first sentence. Concrete over abstract: name the thing, the number, the person who acts.

**Warnings** (product equivalents: error messages, destructive-action confirms, billing notices). Warning before the action, never after. Condition first, then command. Say what happens: "This cannot be undone" beats "Proceed with caution." One warning, one hazard.

**Punctuation.** Standard only — no em-dash chains, no ellipses for tone, no exclamation marks. Hyphenate compound modifiers ("two-way sync"). No slash; "and/or" is banned. Serial comma always.

**Practices.** Spell out abbreviations on first use, then stay consistent. Digits for numbers in technical contexts. Unambiguous dates (spell the month). Units every time. Same word order across parallel list items.

## Word Swaps (High-Frequency)

Full tables in `references/word-swaps.md`. The constant offenders:

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| utilize, employ | use |
| ensure, verify, confirm | make sure |
| perform, execute, conduct | do |
| commence, initiate, launch (a process) | start |
| terminate, cease, halt | stop |
| obtain, acquire | get |
| assist, facilitate | help |
| indicate, display | show |
| modify, alter, adjust | change |
| permit, allow, enable | let |
| leverage, harness, unlock, empower | rewrite the sentence |
| prior to / subsequent to | before / after |
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| in the event that | if |
| approximately | about |
| numerous, multiple | many, or the number |
| the majority of | most |
| substantially, significantly | give the number |
| solution, offering | the product name, or what it is |
| functionality, capability | what it does, as a verb |

**Banned marketing vocabulary** (says nothing, so the reader supplies nothing): seamless, frictionless, effortless, robust, powerful, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, best-in-class, world-class, industry-leading, next-generation, revolutionary, game-changing, disruptive, innovative, transformative, unparalleled, turnkey, holistic, synergy, scalable (without a number), enterprise-grade, blazing-fast, AI-powered (unless the AI is the product), supercharge, unlock, unleash, empower, elevate, reimagine, redefine, craft (as verb), delight (as verb), journey (non-literal), magic, magical, simply, just, easily, effortlessly.

**Hedges and filler — delete on sight:** very, really, quite, rather, fairly, somewhat, actually, basically, essentially, literally, obviously, clearly, of course, that being said, at the end of the day, when it comes to, in terms of, the fact that.

**Modals:** can (ability), must (mandatory), "we recommend that you..." (recommended), do not (prohibited), will (future fact). Avoid shall, should, may, might — all ambiguous.

**Fine as-is:** technical names ("rate plan", "webhook", "API key", "booking engine") and plain Anglo-Saxon verbs (get, do, put, make, keep, let, show). Plain is the point.

## Anti-Patterns

- **The bolt-on.** "Automated sync so you can focus on growing your business." Generic benefit that could follow any feature. Delete it or make it specific.
- **The abstraction taken too far.** "Freedom." alone in 96pt type. The primitive must be attached to the thing it frees the reader from, or it is decoration.
- **The feature disguised as a benefit.** "Get powerful insights into your portfolio." Insights are not a primitive. So what? → you see which unit loses money → you fix it → **power**. Write that.
- **Benefit inflation.** Claiming peace of mind for something that half-works. The reader finds out in week two and never trusts the page again.
- **Us-language.** "We built", "our platform", "we are excited to". More "you"s than "we"s, always.

## Checks Before Shipping

1. Does any sentence pass the word cap?
2. Does any word appear in two senses, or any concept under two words?
3. Is there an `-ing`, a passive, or a perfect tense?
4. Is there a stack of four or more nouns?
5. Could a competitor paste any sentence onto their page unchanged? If yes, it says nothing.
6. Does the first sentence of each block land on a primitive, or the concrete thing it removes?
7. Is every claim checkable — a number, a named outcome, a thing that stops happening?
8. Are there more "you"s than "we"s?
9. Did any bullet fail the three-hop chain and survive anyway? Cut it.

## When STE Fights the Brand

It will. STE was built for aircraft maintenance manuals, where a misread sentence kills someone. Marketing copy has a different job. When a rule strips something the copy genuinely needs — rhythm in a headline, one evocative verb that carries the whole page — keep the rule for the body and break it deliberately in that one place. Then say so in the Notes section. Silent deviations turn into drift, and drift is how a codebase of copy ends up with four words for the same thing.
