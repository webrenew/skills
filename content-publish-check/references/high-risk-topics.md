# High-Risk Topics by Industry

A post on health, legal, money or safety can hurt a reader who follows it. These notes say
where to look; they are not legal advice. When the business's own adviser, board or
compliance contact gives a stricter rule, that rule wins. Record the reviewer in the approval
log.

## For every high-risk post

- A qualified reviewer, named on the post with their credential.
- A link to the authority for each rule or number (regulator, code, official guidance).
- A "when to call a licensed professional" section.
- No promise of a result. Describe what usually happens and what changes it.
- The publish date and the review date, visible on the page.

## Health: dental, medical, chiropractic, therapy, med spa, pharmacy

- Health claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence (FTC Health Products
  Compliance Guidance, 2022). Cite the study or the professional body, not a blog.
- Patient stories, photos, before-and-after images and testimonials need written HIPAA
  authorization for marketing use. Removing the name is not enough when the face or the case
  can identify the patient.
- Many state dental and medical boards limit the word "specialist" to recognized specialties
  and to providers who hold them. Check the state board's advertising rules.
- No diagnosis in a post. "These signs mean you should see a dentist this week" is fine;
  "this is an abscess" is not.
- Results vary: before-and-after photos show typical results, or say clearly that they do not.

## Legal: attorneys

- State bar rules, most based on ABA Model Rules 7.1 to 7.3, ban false or misleading
  statements. A past result can mislead when it suggests the reader will get the same.
- Many states require a label ("Attorney Advertising") or a disclaimer on case results.
  Check the state's rules.
- "Specialist" or "certified" only with a certification the state recognizes.
- No client names or case details without the client's written consent. Never anything
  covered by confidentiality.
- Say that a post is general information, not legal advice, and that no attorney-client
  relationship starts from reading it.

## Money: tax, accounting, financial advice, insurance, lending

- Investment advisers: the SEC Marketing Rule limits testimonials, endorsements and
  performance figures, and requires disclosures. Broker-dealers: FINRA Rule 2210.
- Tax practitioners: Treasury Circular 230 (section 10.30) bans false or misleading
  statements in solicitations.
- Insurance and lending: state insurance departments and lending rules set ad requirements.
- Use figures from the official source (IRS, the state, the regulator), with the tax year.
- Have the business's compliance contact approve the post.

## Safety: electrical, gas, roofing, tree work, pest control, pools, structural

- No DIY steps for gas lines, electrical panels and circuits, structural changes, roof work
  at height, tree felling near lines, or pesticide mixing beyond the label. Explain the risk,
  then stop.
- Name the permit or code that applies (for example the local building department, the NEC
  for electrical work) and link to the local office.
- Pest control: "The label is the law" (EPA). Never advise a use the product label does not
  allow.
- Some states require the contractor licence number in every advertisement (California is one
  example). Check whether a post counts as an ad in the business's state, and add the number
  if in doubt.

## Food and restaurants

- "Gluten-free" on a US label means under 20 ppm gluten (FDA). A restaurant post that says
  "gluten-free" must be able to support that.
- Allergen statements come from the kitchen's actual process, confirmed by the owner.
- No health claims for dishes or ingredients.

## Real estate and housing

- Fair housing rules ban ads that show a preference based on protected classes (race, color,
  religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, and more under state law).
  Describe the property, not who it suits: "three bedrooms near two parks", not "perfect for
  young families".

## Children and schools: childcare, tutoring, camps

- No child's name, face or story without a parent's written consent.
- No promise of a result (grades, test scores, admissions).
